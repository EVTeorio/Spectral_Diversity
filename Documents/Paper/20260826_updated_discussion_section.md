## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Scale Structures The Spectral-Biodiversity Relationship

The central pattern in this study is that relationships between PCA-based canopy spectral heterogeneity and biodiversity strengthened as quadrat grain increased from 10 m to 50 m. This scale dependence suggests that the Spectral Variation Hypothesis is not only a question of whether spectral heterogeneity can represent biodiversity, but also a question of whether the spatial grain of the spectral measurement matches the spatial grain at which canopy biodiversity is expressed.

At 10 m, each quadrat captures a relatively small portion of the canopy. Spectral values at this grain are likely influenced by individual crowns, within-crown variation, small canopy gaps, local shadow geometry, crown-edge effects, geolocation mismatch between field and imagery data, and a limited number of canopy species or individuals. These factors can add substantial fine-scale spectral variation that is not necessarily tied to quadrat-level biodiversity. In that setting, the biological signal exists inside a noisy local spectral field.

At broader grains, quadrats average over more crowns, canopy positions, and local illumination conditions. This averaging does not remove biological variation; instead, it reduces the dominance of single-crown or single-pixel effects and allows spectral summaries to better represent the integrated canopy community. Because phylogenetic diversity and abundance-weighted Faith's PD are community-level metrics, stronger relationships at 20 m and especially 50 m are consistent with broader quadrats better matching the spatial scale of the biodiversity metrics.

The 50 m relationships therefore represent the clearest scale-matched expression of the spectral-biodiversity signal in the current analysis. The 20 m grain remains important because it provides an intermediate compromise between ecological integration and sample size. The 10 m grain is still informative, but it appears to be a scale where local canopy and illumination processes can obscure the community-level relationship.

### 4.2 Metric Construction Changes What Spectral Heterogeneity Means

The three forward PCA-based spectral metrics are related because they are all derived from the same retained pixel cloud in PCA space, but they emphasize different properties of that cloud. Mean Euclidean distance summarizes the average distance of pixels from the quadrat spectral centroid in PC1-PC3 space. It is a centroid-based measure of typical spectral dispersion and is comparatively stable because each pixel contributes linearly to the mean.

Spectral Rao's Q also uses PC1-PC3 space, but it uses squared Euclidean distance. Even though the implementation uses the equivalent centroid formula, the metric conceptually represents an equal-weight pairwise squared-distance diversity measure. Squaring distances gives greater leverage to pixels that are far from the quadrat centroid. This makes Rao's Q theoretically appealing as a diversity metric, but also more sensitive to rare spectral observations, residual illumination, canopy edges, unusual leaf angles, gaps, or small artifacts that occupy the tails of the spectral distribution.

Alpha-hull area uses PC1-PC2 space and describes the two-dimensional area occupied by the quadrat spectral cloud. Unlike mean distance, which summarizes typical dispersion, alpha hull is a boundary-based metric. It can therefore capture the spectral envelope of the quadrat, including less common spectral positions that may correspond to rare canopy species, phylogenetically distinct species, or unusual crown conditions. This same property also makes alpha hull more sensitive to extreme pixel values and to choices made during point sampling or hull construction.

The stronger performance of standardized PCA alpha hull suggests that the occupied spectral envelope may capture more biodiversity realted information that average-distance metrics partly smooth over. If phylogenetic diversity is expressed through the presence of canopy lineages with distinct spectral traits, a hull-based metric can respond to those outer portions of spectral space. However, this interpretation requires caution because hull metrics do not distinguish biologically meaningful spectral extremes from extremes caused by illumination, topography, or canopy structure. The value of alpha hull in this study is therefore not that it is universally superior, but that it provides a complementary view of spectral heterogeneity that appears especially sensitive to the multivariate spectral spread associated with phylogenetic composition.

### 4.3 Why Phylogenetic Diversity Relates To Spectral Heterogeneity More Than Shannon Diversity

The contrast between phylogenetic metrics and conventional species-diversity metrics is one of the most important biological results. Shannon diversity, Simpson diversity, species richness, and evenness summarize how many species occur in a quadrat and how their canopy abundance is distributed. They do not retain information about which species are present or how evolutionarily distant those species are from one another. A quadrat containing several closely related canopy species and a quadrat containing several distantly related canopy species can therefore have similar Shannon or richness values while differing substantially in evolutionary composition.

Phylogenetic metrics keep part of that biological identity. Faith's PD records the branch length represented by the species present. Phylogenetic Rao's Q combines species abundance with phylogenetic distances among species. Abundance-weighted Faith's PD adds crown-overlap abundance to branch-length representation. These metrics can respond to differences in lineage composition that species-count or evenness metrics ignore.

This distinction matters because spectra are not expected to track species diversity in a purely numerical sense. Tree canopies are constrained by shared physiological functions. Leaves must capture light, regulate water, maintain structure, and support photosynthesis; as a result, most healthy canopy vegetation is broadly green and shares common spectral features such as visible pigment absorption, a green reflectance peak, red-edge behavior, and strong near-infrared scattering. These shared constraints limit the amount of spectral divergence among species.

At the same time, species are not spectrally identical. Evolutionary divergence can be associated with differences in leaf pigments, water content, internal leaf structure, leaf mass per area, phenology, crown architecture, and canopy organization. Closely related species may share more of these traits, while distantly related species may differ more strongly in the traits that shape reflectance. Under this interpretation, phylogenetic diversity can show weak-to-moderate relationships with spectral heterogeneity because it partly represents trait and lineage differences that influence canopy spectra. Shannon diversity can remain near zero because it treats species identities as interchangeable.

### 4.4 Biodiversity Metrics Differ Because They Encode Different Biological Information

The biodiversity metrics used in this study differ in three main ways: whether they count species, whether they include abundance structure, and whether they include evolutionary history. Species richness is a count of species with positive canopy overlap. Shannon and Simpson diversity use proportional crown-overlap abundance and are therefore sensitive to dominance and evenness. Species evenness standardizes Shannon diversity by richness and focuses more directly on abundance balance.

Faith's PD is presence-based and therefore tends to track richness because adding species often adds branches to the phylogeny. However, Faith's PD is not equivalent to richness because the amount of branch length added depends on where a species sits in the tree. Phylogenetic Rao's Q and abundance-weighted Faith's PD differ more strongly from richness and Shannon because they incorporate both abundance and evolutionary structure. These metrics ask not only how many species are present, but whether the canopy is dominated by closely related or distantly related lineages.

Crown-equivalent individuals also affect metric behavior. Because the abundance matrix is based on crown overlap rather than stem count, biodiversity metrics are weighted toward the canopy material visible to the sensor. This is important for connecting field biodiversity to hyperspectral imagery, but it also means that large crowns and dominant canopy species can influence abundance-weighted phylogenetic metrics differently than they influence species richness.

These differences explain why biodiversity metrics do not produce identical spectral relationships. Species-diversity metrics mainly summarize richness and dominance. Phylogenetic metrics preserve information about evolutionary distance and lineage composition. The spectral signal appears more closely tied to the latter, especially when canopy composition is aggregated across broader quadrats.

### 4.5 Vector Normalization Reduces Brightness Dominance And Preserves Spectral Shape

The comparison between raw PCA and vector-normalized PCA clarifies why preprocessing choices matter. In the raw PCA, PC1 was strongly associated with overall mean reflectance, indicating that the first raw PCA axis was largely a brightness axis. This is expected in reflectance data because illumination, canopy openness, leaf angle, shadow, and topography can create broad changes in reflectance magnitude across many wavelengths.

Vector normalization scaled each retained pixel spectrum to unit length before PCA. This reduced the dominance of overall brightness magnitude and shifted the standardized PCA toward differences in spectral shape. The standardized PCA still retained some brightness and illumination structure, but it no longer allowed broad reflectance magnitude to dominate PC1 in the same way as the raw PCA.

This distinction helps explain why vector-normalized PCA metrics generally related more strongly to phylogenetic diversity than raw PCA metrics. If phylogenetic signal is expressed through subtle differences in spectral shape rather than simple brightness, reducing the brightness axis should make the PCA space more biologically interpretable. In that sense, vector normalization does not remove all nonbiological variation, but it improves the chance that distance, dispersion, and hull area in PCA space reflect leaf and canopy spectral traits rather than illumination intensity alone.

### 4.6 Spectral Drivers Reflect Both Biology And Measurement Geometry

The metric-driver analysis indicates that standardized PCA heterogeneity metrics remain related to reflectance properties in visible and near-infrared regions, as well as elevation. This is not a flaw in the analysis; it is part of what spectral heterogeneity means. Pixel reflectance values are the raw material from which all spectral metrics are calculated. Differences among metrics arise from how those reflectance values are transformed, projected, summarized, and weighted.

Visible-region brightness can reflect pigment-related variation, canopy illumination, and species or leaf-condition differences. Near-infrared brightness can reflect leaf and canopy structure, scattering, shadow, and crown geometry. Elevation can affect spectral heterogeneity indirectly through species composition, canopy structure, moisture, topographic exposure, or illumination geometry. Because these factors overlap in real canopy imagery, spectral heterogeneity should be interpreted as an integrated canopy signal rather than a direct measurement of biodiversity alone.

Metric sensitivity to extreme values is central here. Mean distance gives each retained pixel a linear contribution to the quadrat average. Rao's Q squares distances and therefore amplifies pixels farther from the centroid. Alpha hull is defined by the boundary of the point cloud and can be affected by the most spectrally peripheral retained pixels. These differences mean that the same reflectance values can produce different spectral heterogeneity rankings depending on whether the metric emphasizes typical dispersion, squared distance, or occupied spectral envelope.

The stronger alpha-hull relationships with phylogenetic metrics may indicate that phylogenetically distinct canopy composition affects the outer envelope of spectral space. The weaker or less consistent performance of Rao's Q may reflect the greater influence of squared extreme distances, including extremes that are not biological. Mean distance occupies a middle position: it captures broad spectral dispersion while dampening the leverage of rare spectral outliers.

### 4.7 Why The Biodiversity-Squared Transformation Helps More At Smaller Scales

The biodiversity-squared transformation changed the smaller-scale relationships more than the largest-scale relationships. This pattern is consistent with a nonlinear relationship between biodiversity and spectral heterogeneity at finer grains. At 10 m, many quadrats likely contain few canopy species or are dominated by one or a few crowns. In this setting, biodiversity values can be compressed over a narrower range, while spectral values remain strongly affected by local crown geometry, illumination, and within-crown variation. Squaring the biodiversity axis stretches differences among higher-biodiversity quadrats and can make a nonlinear or threshold-like pattern more visible.

At broader scales, quadrats already integrate more individuals, species, and canopy conditions. This aggregation can make the relationship between biodiversity and spectral heterogeneity more linear because local noise is averaged and community composition is represented more completely. As a result, the transformation has less room to improve the strongest 50 m relationships. The transformation results therefore support the same scale interpretation: fine-scale spectral-biodiversity relationships are noisier and more nonlinear, while broader grains produce a more stable community-level association.

The transformation should not be interpreted as evidence that squared biodiversity is inherently more biological than the original metrics. Instead, it is a sensitivity result showing that the relationship between biodiversity and spectral heterogeneity may be nonlinear at small grains. The untransformed metrics remain the clearest primary reporting scale because they preserve the direct interpretation of the biodiversity values.

### 4.8 Spatial Structure And Remaining Caution

Spatial autocorrelation is an important caution for these results. The priority spectral and biodiversity variables showed spatial structure, and residual spatial structure remained in several relationships, especially at 10 m and 20 m. This is expected in a contiguous forest plot because nearby quadrats share canopy composition, topography, illumination conditions, and spatially structured environmental gradients.

The reduction of residual spatial structure in several 50 m phylogenetic relationships suggests that broader grains may better capture the spatially structured canopy-biodiversity signal. However, the 50 m scale also has the smallest number of quadrats, so stronger correlations at that scale should be interpreted as evidence of scale dependence rather than as final predictive performance. Future spatial models or spatial cross-validation would strengthen inference by separating spatially shared structure from independent spectral-biodiversity association.

### 4.9 Implications For Remote Sensing Of Biodiversity

Together, these findings show that both metric selection and spatial scale influence the ability of hyperspectral imagery to detect biodiversity structure. PCA-based spectral heterogeneity was most informative when biodiversity was represented using phylogenetic metrics rather than conventional species-diversity summaries, and the strongest relationships occurred at broader quadrat grains.

The results also show that no single spectral metric captures the full biodiversity signal. Standardized PCA alpha hull appears sensitive to the occupied spectral envelope, standardized PCA mean Euclidean distance provides a stable centroid-based measure of average spectral dispersion, and standardized PCA Rao's Q provides a theory-linked squared-distance measure that may be more sensitive to spectral extremes. These differences should be treated as useful information rather than as simple metric competition. They show that spectral heterogeneity is not one quantity; it is a family of related summaries that emphasize different parts of canopy spectral variation.

The scale dependence of the spectral-phylogenetic relationship indicates that biodiversity assessments based on hyperspectral imagery should explicitly evaluate spatial grain when selecting analytical methods. A single resolution is unlikely to be optimal across ecosystems, canopy structures, or biodiversity metrics. Instead, spectral diversity metrics should be evaluated at grains that correspond to the ecological organization of the community and to the field metrics being compared.

Future work integrating functional trait measurements, species-level spectral libraries, environmental covariates, and spatially explicit validation will help determine the relative contributions of evolutionary history, canopy structure, illumination, topography, and species composition to observed spectral heterogeneity. Such analyses will improve the interpretation of hyperspectral data as a tool for monitoring forest biodiversity across landscapes while preserving the central caution from this study: spectral heterogeneity is an informative but indirect biodiversity signal whose meaning depends on scale, preprocessing, and metric construction.
